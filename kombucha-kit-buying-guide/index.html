How to choose the right kombucha kit

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

A good kombucha kit should include a glass jar (at least 1 gallon, wide-mouth), breathable cover, starter tea, and a healthy SCOBY. Skip fancy add-ons if you are new; instead focus on quality materials and ease of cleaning. Most home brewers find that a simple kit paired with a few cloth covers and rubber bands delivers the best value without overcomplicating the process.

What to look for in a kombucha brewing kit

A kombucha kit is really only a few things: a vessel, a cover, and the starting culture. The vessel should be glass – never plastic or metal for the fermentation itself. Look for a wide mouth (at least 5 inches across) so you can reach in to clean and remove the SCOBY. Most starter kits use a 1-gallon jar, which is perfect for a first batch of about 3 quarts of finished tea. The cover matters too. You want a tightly woven cloth that lets air flow but keeps out fruit flies and dust. Many kits include a paper filter or a muslin cloth, but owners report that thin fabric can allow tiny insects through. A tight-weave cotton cover or a purpose-made mesh lid with a flip-up spout is worth upgrading. Finally, the starter kit should include enough starter tea (unflavored, already fermented) to acidify your first batch – at least 1 cup per gallon of sweet tea.

Glass vs. plastic jars – which is better?

Glass wins hands-down for kombucha brewing. Plastic can harbor scratches that hide bacteria and may leach chemicals, especially with the acidic tea and long ferment times. Ceramic crocks are fine if the glaze is food-safe, but they are harder to find in starter kits. Glass lets you see the SCOBY grow and check for mold without opening the jar. Some budget kits sell a plastic brewing jar, but experienced home brewers almost always recommend replacing it with a glass one. If you already have a glass jar at home, you can often buy just the SCOBY and starter tea separately – that can be cheaper than a full kit and gives you a better vessel out of the gate.

Do you need a kit with a spigot?

A spigot (tap) on the jar makes bottling easier, especially if you brew in a counter-height location. You can fill bottles without lifting the heavy jar or siphoning. The trade-off is cleaning: spigots have small parts where mold and yeast can hide. Many home brewers report that spigot assemblies must be disassembled and scrubbed after every batch, or at least rinsed with boiling water before each use. If you are a beginner and value convenience over extra cleaning, a spigot kit can save a few minutes per bottling day. Just make sure the spigot is stainless steel or food-grade plastic that doesn’t impart flavors. For pure simplicity, a jar without a spigot is fine – you can pour or use a ladle.

Essential accessories: starter tea, SCOBY, and heat wraps

Not every kit includes a SCOBY (the pancake-like culture). Many sell the jar and a bottle of starter tea, expecting you to know someone who has a SCOBY. Look for a kit that explicitly includes both a healthy SCOBY and at least one cup of strong starter tea. Without the starter tea, your first batch may not acidify quickly enough and can invite mold. A heat wrap is not essential for most homes, but if your house runs below 70°F, fermentation will slow. A few kits include a low-wattage heating pad designed to wrap around the jar. Owners who brew in cool basements or drafty apartments find it helps keep fermentation steady. You can always buy a separate heat wrap later if needed. Also, make sure the kit comes with a simple instruction booklet – the best ones include a troubleshooting chart for common issues like mold or weak fizz.

How much space does brewing require?

A typical 1-gallon jar is about 8 inches tall and 6 inches wide – it fits on a kitchen counter or a small shelf. You need room above the jar for the cloth cover to dome up as the SCOBY grows. Also, the jar should be away from direct sunlight and drafts. Many brewers keep their jar on a towel or a small tray to catch drips from the spigot or condensation. For second fermentation (bottling and carbonating) you will need a few flip-top glass bottles – that adds perhaps another 1 square foot of counter space. Overall, you can start brewing in an apartment kitchen with minimal footprint. If you plan to brew continuously, you may want a second jar so you can start a new batch while the first one is bottled. However, a single-kit setup works fine for most households.

Common mistakes beginners make and how kits help

The top three mistakes reported by home brewers are: using too little starter tea (the batch goes moldy), washing the jar with soap (residue kills the SCOBY), and fermenting in a location that is too hot or too cold. A good kit helps by providing enough starter tea and explicitly warning against soap in the instructions. Some kits include a pH test strip – using that is a smart way to confirm your brew is acidic enough. Another mistake is overcomplicating the first batch – adding fruit or herbs too early. Kits that encourage you to keep it simple (just tea, sugar, starter tea, and the SCOBY) for the first two weeks tend to yield better results. Look for a kit whose brand has a reputation for responsive customer support; if you hit a problem, an email or chat might save the batch.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to brew kombucha from a kit?

The first fermentation (making the tea sour and fizzy) usually takes 7 to 14 days, depending on temperature and how strong you like it. A second fermentation, where you add fruit or flavorings in sealed bottles, takes another 2 to 4 days. Kit instructions often suggest tasting after day 7.

Can I reuse my SCOBY for multiple batches?

Yes, the SCOBY will grow a new, rubbery layer on top with every batch. You can keep using the layers indefinitely as long as they stay healthy and free of mold. Many home brewers maintain a continuous SCOBY hotel for years.

Do I need special bottles for the second fermentation?

Flip-top (Grolsch-style) glass bottles with a rubber gasket are ideal because they seal tightly and let you control carbonation. Used kombucha bottles with threaded caps can work, but they may not hold pressure as well, risking weak fizz or messy leaks.

What size kit should a beginner start with?

A 1-gallon kit is the standard starting point – it produces about three 16-ounce bottles per batch. Larger kits (2 gallons or more) are only needed if you drink a lot or want to give away bottles. A smaller jar makes it easier to adjust and clean while you learn.

How do I clean the jar between batches?

Rinse the jar with hot water and a non-abrasive sponge. Never use soap, as residue can harm the SCOBY. For tougher spots, a mixture of white vinegar and baking soda works well. Some experienced brewers give the jar a quick rinse with distilled white vinegar before refilling.

Is a full kombucha kit worth it compared to buying just the SCOBY?

If you already have a clean, wide-mouth glass jar and a piece of cloth, buying just a SCOBY and starter tea is cheaper. But a full kit saves hassle if you are new – it ensures the right jar, proper cover, and reliable starter liquid. Many beginners find the upfront cost worthwhile for the convenience and confidence.

In shortA kombucha kit is a smart entry point if you want to test the brewing waters without tracking down supplies piecemeal. Focus on glass, a tight-weave cover, and a kit that includes both a healthy SCOBY and ample starter tea. Keep your first batch simple, clean with hot water only, and you will be sipping your own fizzy, tangy kombucha a few weeks later.
